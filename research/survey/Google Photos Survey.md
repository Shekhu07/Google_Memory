# Google Photos Survey
## Finding an old photo when you do not remember the details

## Purpose

This survey supports the Google Photos fellowship case study. It focuses on one specific problem: **finding an old photo when you remember the moment but not the exact date, place, album, or words to search for.**

The survey is designed to be quick and easy. It should take **5–7 minutes**. Most questions use simple multiple-choice answers. Respondents should not share photos, names, account details, addresses, or medical information.

## Google Forms setup

| Setting | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Target respondents | People who have used Google Photos in the last 6 months |
| Target sample | 50–100 responses |
| Follow-up research | Select 5–6 people for short interviews |
| Required questions | Only mark screening and key behavior questions as required |
| Question order | Real experience first, Ask Photos next, idea reaction last |
| Contact details | Collect in a separate form, not with survey answers |

## Form title

**How do you find old photos in Google Photos?**

## Form description

> We are learning how people find old photos and videos when they remember the moment but not the exact details. This survey takes about 5–7 minutes. Please do not share any private information or upload photos. There are no right or wrong answers.

# Analysis plan

## What the survey should help establish

The survey is successful if it helps answer four simple questions:

1. **Who struggles most?** For example, users with large libraries or frequent travel and document memories.
2. **What do they remember and forget?** This identifies the memory cues that could support retrieval.
3. **Where does retrieval fail?** The main breakdown may be expression, understanding, result evaluation, or refinement after a miss.
4. **What do users do instead?** Workarounds reveal the cost of the problem and help identify the opportunity.

## Simple retrieval outcome split

Use Q11 to create three groups:

| Group | Q11 responses |
|---|---|
| Successful | Found exact item; found after trying more than once |
| Uncertain | Found something similar but was not sure; found surrounding event only |
| Failed | Could not find it; stopped looking |

The **uncertain** group is important. They may be the clearest opportunity because the product surfaces relevant content but does not help users recognize or confirm the right moment.

## Root-cause comparison

Use Q13 to compare the four possible failure points in the fellowship brief:

| Possible problem | Survey answer |
|---|---|
| User cannot express the memory | “I did not know what to type or say” |
| Product does not understand the clues | “Google Photos did not understand what I meant” |
| Results are hard to evaluate | “I could not tell which result was right” |
| User cannot refine a failed attempt | “I did not know how to continue after a wrong result” |

Compare each problem by outcome, time spent, repeated attempts, and workarounds—not only by how many people selected it.

## Ask Photos comparison

Separate respondents into four groups:

1. Have not heard of Ask Photos.
2. Have heard of it but have not used it.
3. Used it and found it useful.
4. Used it but remained uncertain or unsuccessful.

The purpose is not to prove Ask Photos is bad. It is to learn which parts of vague-memory retrieval it already handles and which parts remain unresolved.

## Choosing the target segment

Use these signals together:

- The person has experienced vague-memory retrieval failure.
- The problem happens more than once.
- The person spends time or tries multiple paths.
- The person uses a workaround.
- The person ends with an uncertain or failed outcome.
- The person can describe a real task for a follow-up interview.

Do not choose a segment only because it is easy to build for. Choose the segment with the clearest repeated problem and meaningful user cost.

## Selecting 5–6 interviews

Choose a mix of users with different journeys:

- One person who eventually found the item after many attempts.
- One person who found a likely item but was not sure.
- One person who failed completely.
- One person who used a workaround.
- One Ask Photos user who succeeded.
- One Ask Photos user who remained uncertain or unsuccessful.

In the interview, ask the participant to walk through the actual episode. Focus on what they remembered first, what they tried, what they expected to happen, what made a result believable, and what they did after a near miss.

## Metrics to carry into MVP testing

The survey is self-reported, so use it to decide what to measure in the prototype:

- Exact retrieval rate.
- Uncertain retrieval rate.
- Failed retrieval rate.
- Time to first useful result.
- Number of attempts or reformulations.
- Near-miss recovery rate.
- Workaround rate.
- Trust in the result.

## Google Forms checklist

- [ ] Keep the form to 5–7 minutes.
- [ ] Use simple language and one idea per question.
- [ ] Keep answer options short.
- [ ] Limit checkbox questions to two selections where noted.
- [ ] Keep open-text questions optional.
- [ ] Show only relevant follow-up questions through branching.
- [ ] Put Memory Trails questions at the end.
- [ ] Do not collect private photos or account details.
- [ ] Use a separate form for interview contact details.
- [ ] Pilot with 3 people before sharing widely.
- [ ] Remove or rewrite any question that respondents misunderstand during the pilot.

## Final guardrail

Do not summarize the finding as:

> “People find it hard to search for old photos.”

The survey should help produce a more specific finding, such as:

> “When people remember a trip or event but not the date or exact place, they often find related photos but remain unsure which moment is correct. They browse nearby photos or ask someone else for help, because the current experience does not make it easy to evaluate or refine a near miss.”
